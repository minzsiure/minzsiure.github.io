## About Me

I am a 3rd year PhD candidate in Neuroscience at the Princeton Neuroscience Institute, supported by a Centennial Fellowship. My research seeks to understand <b>the neural basis of decision-making</b>, spanning levels of analysis from circuit-specific mechanisms to brain-wide characterization. I approach these questions primarily from a computational perspective, with theoretical insights directly tested and refined through cellular-level experiments. These days, I dive into designing new animal behaviors and collecting electrophysiological data myself to better connect with experiments as a theorist. I am primarily advised by <a href="https://pni.princeton.edu/people/carlos-brody" targert=_blank>Carlos Brody</a>, and co-advised by <a href="https://psychology.princeton.edu/people/nathaniel-daw" targert=_blank>Nathaniel Daw</a> and <a href="https://ben-eysenbach.github.io/" targert=_blank>Ben Eysenbach</a>. I am a visiting scientist in <a href="https://alleninstitute.org/person/stefan-mihalas/" target=_blank> Stefan Mihalas</a>' Theory & Modeling group within the Allen Institute.

## Research
Broadly, I view cognition as a computational process grounded in the basis of neurons and synapses, and I believe we can gain insights into these processes through models of neural networks. With the very recent development of large-scale neurophysiological recordings and connectome datasets, we now have unprecedented opportunities to investigate: <b><em>How do multiple brain regions interact to support decision-making? </em></b> To that end, my computational works span the following three aspects, in complement to my ongoing development of new behavioral tasks in rats for studying how the brain detect and incorporate statistical structures into decision-making. 
<!-- Recordings in our lab have revealed that information about animals' decisions is widespread across the brain <a href="https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.08.21.609044v7" target="_blank">[Bondy*, Charlton*, Luo* et al., 2026]</a>, yet this tells us little about the coordination of different brain regions. Our lack of deeper insights may be due to traditional analyses developed for single-region studies that are not well suited to multi-region questions, as well as the simplicity of the animal tasks being studied. -->

1. <b>Normative</b>: Developing biologically plausible models of interacting brain regions that perform brain-like computations, to uncover principles of neural coding and information flow that can be directly verified with neurophysiology experiments <a href="https://minzsiure.github.io/multiregion-brain-model/" target="_blank">[<b>Yi Xie</b>, Jaedong Hwang, Carlos Brody, David Tank, Ila Fiete; ICML 2025]</a>. This work is currently being experimentally validated, which we will present preliminary results at SfN 2026.

2. <b>Mechanistic</b>: Analyzing networks with biologically plausible properties, such as theoretically underexplored heavy-tailed synaptic weight distributions <a href="https://openreview.net/forum?id=J0SbYYY0po" target="_blank">[<b>Eva Yi Xie</b>, Stefan Mihalas, Łukasz Kuśmierz; NeurIPS 2025] to reveal the dynamics and info-processing constraints imposed by connectivity structure alone. We are actively extending this work to make predictions and validations, utilizing connectomic and whole-brain imaging data in fruit flies.

3. <b>Descriptive</b>: Creating computational tools to decode multi-region coordinated neural signals and capture the moment-to-moment computations animals perform when making decisions (<em>e.g., When precisely do animals make up their mind?</em>) <a href="#" style="pointer-events:none; cursor:default; text-decoration:none;">[<b>Ongoing</b>]</a>.

Together, these efforts aim to advance our understanding of multi-region neural computation and may also offer useful insights for developing AI systems that reflect key properties of the brain—such as robustness and efficiency.
